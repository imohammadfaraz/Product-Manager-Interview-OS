# PM Interview Handbook

## Purpose

A Product Manager interview is a set of conversations designed to understand how you think, make decisions, communicate, and work with others. The exact format differs by company, role, product, and level. Treat this handbook as a preparation map, not a prediction of a specific employer's process.

## 1. Map the interview before you study

Ask the recruiter or hiring contact, politely and directly:

- What rounds are planned, and what is each round intended to assess?
- Will there be a case, presentation, take-home exercise, SQL task, or technical discussion?
- Who will interview me: hiring manager, peer PM, engineering, design, data, or leadership?
- What is the expected duration and format?
- Is there anything I should prepare or bring?

Record only what you are told. Do not assume a process based on another candidate's experience.

## 2. Common interview round categories

| Round | Typical focus | How to prepare |
|---|---|---|
| Recruiter screen | Motivation, role fit, communication, logistics | Prepare a 60-90 second introduction and a clear reason for the role. |
| Hiring manager | Scope, ownership, outcomes, product judgment | Prepare two projects in depth, including decisions and results. |
| Product Sense / Design | User understanding, problem framing, solutions | Practise improving or designing products under a timebox. |
| Strategy | Market, positioning, growth, business model, choices | Prepare market and competitive reasoning, not company trivia. |
| Execution | Prioritisation, delivery, dependencies, risk | Practise making decisions with limited capacity and changing constraints. |
| Metrics / Analytics | Measurement, diagnosis, experiments, data reasoning | Build metric trees and investigate metric changes systematically. |
| Technical | Systems, APIs, constraints, engineering partnership | Explain concepts accurately and connect them to user and business outcomes. |
| Behavioural / Leadership | Ownership, influence, conflict, learning | Build a specific story bank and practise concise storytelling. |
| Presentation / Case | Synthesis, recommendation, communication | State the decision early, support it with evidence, and explain risks. |

## 3. What interviewers need to see

A candidate should make their thinking observable. Across rounds, work to demonstrate:

1. **Customer understanding:** You can identify whose problem matters and why.
2. **Business understanding:** You can connect product work to sustainable outcomes.
3. **Structured reasoning:** You can break an ambiguous problem into manageable parts.
4. **Prioritisation:** You can choose what matters now and explain what you will not do.
5. **Execution:** You can turn a decision into a sequence of work, owners, and checkpoints.
6. **Analytical judgment:** You can select useful measures and interpret evidence carefully.
7. **Influence:** You can align people without relying only on authority.
8. **Communication:** You can be concise, transparent, and responsive to new information.

## 4. Adjust your evidence to the level

The same competency looks different at different levels. An aspiring PM can demonstrate it through a personal project, internship, volunteering, operations work, or a well-reasoned exercise. An experienced PM should demonstrate ownership, outcomes, and trade-offs from real work.

| Level | What to make visible |
|---|---|
| Aspiring / APM | User curiosity, structured problem solving, learning speed, clear communication, reliable execution on a scoped problem. |
| PM | Ownership of a problem area, discovery, prioritisation, delivery, metrics, and cross-functional collaboration. |
| Senior PM | Ambiguous problem selection, strategy, multi-team alignment, measurable business impact, and coaching or leverage. |
| Lead / Group PM | Portfolio choices, organisational alignment, resource allocation, leadership through other PMs, and durable operating mechanisms. |

Do not inflate your role. Explain exactly what you owned, what others owned, and what changed because of your contribution.

## 5. A reliable structure for ambiguous questions

Use this sequence as a thinking aid. Adapt it to the prompt; do not recite it mechanically.

1. **Clarify:** Ask only the questions that could change your approach. Identify user, objective, scope, time horizon, and constraints.
2. **State assumptions:** If information is unavailable, state a reasonable assumption and proceed. Invite correction.
3. **Frame:** Explain the problem in one sentence and define what success means.
4. **Explore:** Segment users, inspect causes, or generate options as appropriate.
5. **Choose:** Prioritise one direction using explicit criteria.
6. **Plan:** Describe the first practical steps, dependencies, and risks.
7. **Measure:** Define a primary outcome, guardrails, and a review window.
8. **Adapt:** Explain what evidence would make you change course.

## 6. How to clarify without stalling

Good clarifying questions reduce material uncertainty. Examples:

- "Are we optimising for new-user activation or long-term retention?"
- "Is this for consumers, merchants, or both?"
- "Are we discussing one city or a national rollout?"
- "Is the launch date fixed, or can scope change?"

Avoid asking a long list of questions whose answers would not change your recommendation. If the interviewer says to make assumptions, state them and move forward.

## 7. How to communicate assumptions

Separate three things:

- **Known:** Information supplied in the prompt.
- **Assumed:** A temporary working premise.
- **Needed:** Evidence you would collect before making a real decision.

Example: "The prompt does not specify the customer segment. I will assume the initial target is first-time users in one metro market. I would validate that segment choice with activation and support data before committing to a launch." 

## 8. Avoid common interview mistakes

| Mistake | Better behaviour |
|---|---|
| Jumping to features | Start with user, context, and problem. |
| Listing every possible user | Select a segment and explain why. |
| Name-dropping frameworks | Use a framework only when it improves the reasoning. |
| Giving a solution without trade-offs | Compare options and state what you are not choosing. |
| Treating metrics as a list | Connect a metric to a behaviour and a business outcome. |
| Claiming team results as personal work | State your role, decision, collaboration, and contribution. |
| Hiding uncertainty | State assumptions and explain how you would validate them. |
| Over-answering | Lead with the recommendation, then provide the reasoning. |
| Treating a case like a real project | Respect the timebox and make a reasoned recommendation with limited data. |
| Defending an answer after new evidence | Update your view and explain why. |

## 9. Build a practice system

For every session, record:

- Date and question.
- Skill being practised.
- Time taken.
- What went well, with evidence.
- Where you became vague or jumped to a conclusion.
- One improvement for the next attempt.
- Score by competency.

Review your log every seven days. Repeated patterns matter more than one low score.

## 10. Before the interview

**24 hours before:** Review your story bank, target company research, role requirements, and two weak areas. Do not attempt to memorise dozens of new frameworks.

**15 minutes before:** Check audio, video, internet, notes, water, and the interview link. Keep only a small prompt sheet nearby. Avoid reading a script.

**During the interview:** Listen fully, clarify, think aloud, signpost your answer, and check whether the interviewer wants more depth or a decision.

**After the interview:** Write down the questions while they are fresh, your uncertainty, and what you would improve. Do not share confidential details publicly.

## Practice task

Record a two-minute answer to: "Tell me about yourself and why this Product Manager role makes sense for you." Listen once. Remove generic claims and add evidence: product problems you have worked on, decisions you made, outcomes, and why this role is a credible next step.
