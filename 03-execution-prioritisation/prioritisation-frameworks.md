# Prioritisation Frameworks: How to Use Them Well

A prioritisation framework makes assumptions visible. It does not calculate the objectively correct roadmap. The PM remains responsible for judgment, context, and communication.

## 1. RICE

RICE compares Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort.

**Formula:** (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort

Use it when you can estimate comparable reach and effort across a set of initiatives. Define the time period for reach and the scale for impact before scoring.

Example (illustrative):

| Initiative | Reach / quarter | Impact score | Confidence | Effort (person-weeks) | RICE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A: Simplify onboarding | 2,000 | 2 | 0.8 | 4 | 800 |
| B: Add admin export | 500 | 1 | 0.9 | 2 | 225 |
| C: Improve search | 1,200 | 1.5 | 0.6 | 3 | 360 |

RICE suggests A first under these assumptions. It does not account automatically for compliance obligations, strategic dependencies, or fairness. Review those separately.

## 2. ICE

ICE compares Impact, Confidence, and Ease. It can be useful for a quick experiment backlog when precise estimates are unavailable. Define each scale and use it consistently. It is easy to create false precision with subjective scores.

## 3. MoSCoW

Classify scope as Must have, Should have, Could have, and Won't have for this release. It is useful for scope conversations and release planning. It is not a substitute for deciding which product opportunity matters most.

## 4. Cost of Delay / time criticality

Consider the consequence of waiting: lost revenue, customer harm, missed seasonal opportunity, increased risk, or downstream blockage. Use this when timing materially changes value. Be explicit about the evidence behind urgency.

## 5. Opportunity scoring

Compare importance of a customer outcome with satisfaction in the current experience. It can help surface underserved needs. Be careful with sample quality, survey interpretation, and the difference between stated importance and actual behaviour.

## A practical prioritisation workflow

1. Define the goal and planning horizon.
2. Remove or separately classify mandatory work.
3. Group requests by underlying customer problem.
4. Define comparable criteria and scoring scales.
5. Score options with the people closest to the evidence.
6. Check dependencies, risks, confidence, and strategic fit.
7. Discuss what the scores hide.
8. Make a decision and record what would trigger a revisit.

## Interview warning signs

- Giving a score without defining the scale.
- Comparing ideas with different time horizons.
- Treating reach as guaranteed adoption.
- Ignoring effort uncertainty and dependencies.
- Ranking all work without identifying mandatory constraints.
- Refusing to make a decision because data is incomplete.

In an interview, it is often stronger to say "I would choose A based on these assumptions, and I would validate X before committing" than to pretend the score is exact.
