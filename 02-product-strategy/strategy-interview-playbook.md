# Strategy Interview Playbook

## What the interviewer is looking for

Strategy questions test whether you can choose where to focus and explain why. They often contain incomplete data. Your job is not to invent certainty. It is to make the decision logic visible, identify the evidence needed, and show how you would learn.

## A practical 8-step approach

### 1. Define the strategic decision

Restate the question as a decision. Examples:

- Which customer segment should we prioritise?
- Should we enter a new market?
- Which growth lever should receive investment?
- Should we build, buy, or partner?

Clarify time horizon, geography, business objective, and constraints where relevant.

### 2. Understand the current position

Identify the product's current customers, value proposition, distribution, monetisation, strengths, weaknesses, and constraints. If facts are missing, list the information you would request instead of fabricating it.

### 3. Assess the opportunity

Consider:

- Customer need and willingness to adopt or pay.
- Market size and growth, with clear assumptions.
- Existing alternatives and competitive intensity.
- Distribution access and acquisition economics.
- Regulatory, operational, and technology constraints.
- Strategic fit with existing assets or capabilities.

### 4. Segment the opportunity

A large market is not automatically an attractive market. Split it into reachable segments with meaningfully different needs, buying behaviour, or economics. Choose an initial segment and explain why it is a sensible entry point.

### 5. Generate strategic options

Options may include focusing on a niche, expanding a use case, improving the core product, changing pricing, building partnerships, entering a new geography, or declining the opportunity.

### 6. Compare options

Use explicit criteria such as customer value, size of opportunity, differentiation, time to value, capability fit, financial sustainability, and risk. State which criteria matter most for the decision.

### 7. Make the recommendation

Lead with the choice. Then explain the supporting logic, what you will not do, the resources required, and the key assumptions.

### 8. Define milestones and evidence

Use staged commitments:

- **Validate:** Confirm the problem and demand.
- **Prove:** Test whether the product can deliver value for a narrow segment.
- **Scale:** Expand only when adoption, retention, economics, and operational quality are credible.

## Market sizing without false precision

A market-size estimate is a structured model, not a magic number. Show the formula, inputs, source or assumption for every major input, range, and sensitivity. Separate total market opportunity from the portion you can realistically serve.

## Competitive analysis

Do not compare competitors only by feature count. Compare the job customers hire each alternative to do, the segment served, switching costs, distribution, trust, economics, and reasons customers choose or reject it. Include non-consumption and manual workarounds as alternatives.

## Growth strategy

Diagnose the growth system before proposing a channel. Map acquisition, activation, engagement, retention, referral, and monetisation where relevant. Identify the bottleneck and the evidence supporting it. A growth idea that brings low-intent users may improve acquisition while damaging retention or unit economics.

## Monetisation and pricing

Clarify who receives value, who pays, what they pay for, and how pricing aligns with value. Consider willingness to pay, packaging, variable costs, sales motion, and fairness. Avoid assuming that raising price always increases revenue or that free usage automatically creates a viable funnel.

## Build, buy, or partner

Compare speed, differentiation, control, cost over time, integration effort, security, data rights, reliability, and strategic dependency. A partner can accelerate entry but may constrain the roadmap. Building can create control but consume scarce capacity.

## Common strategy mistakes

- Quoting TAM without explaining the assumptions.
- Confusing a market trend with a customer problem.
- Assuming a competitor's feature list is its strategy.
- Recommending expansion before proving retention or unit economics.
- Ignoring sales, operations, support, compliance, or distribution.
- Offering a vision without a sequence of choices.
- Treating every opportunity as equally attractive.

## Practice deliverable

Choose a product you know. Write a one-page recommendation answering: "Which customer segment should this product serve better over the next 12 months, and what should it stop doing to focus?" Include assumptions, alternatives, milestones, risks, and metrics.
