# Influence, Conflict, and Leadership

## Influence without authority

Influence comes from understanding what matters to others, making the problem clear, building evidence, creating options, and following through. It does not mean persuading everyone to agree with you.

A practical approach:

1. Identify the decision and who owns it.
2. Understand each stakeholder's goal, incentive, and concern.
3. Separate facts from assumptions and opinions.
4. Make the customer and business impact visible.
5. Offer options with consequences, not a single demand.
6. Invite challenge and adjust where evidence warrants it.
7. Confirm the decision, owner, and follow-up.

## Resolve disagreement

When Engineering, Design, Sales, or leadership disagrees with your proposal:

- Ask what risk or goal is driving their position.
- Restate the shared outcome.
- Identify the real decision: scope, timing, quality, customer segment, or investment.
- Use evidence where available.
- Make a reversible test where uncertainty is high.
- Escalate only when a decision cannot be made at the working level or a material risk needs leadership ownership.

## Handle a difficult stakeholder

Do not label someone as "difficult" in an interview and stop there. Explain the behaviour, its impact, what you tried, what changed, and what you learned about the working relationship.

## Leadership under pressure

Show how you maintained clarity when a deadline, incident, customer escalation, or strategic change created stress. Explain how you prioritised, communicated uncertainty, supported the team, and protected customers.

## Failure and learning

Choose a real example where your decision, assumption, or execution was inadequate. Own your part. Explain the impact, what you changed, and evidence that your behaviour improved. Avoid stories where the only lesson is that someone else made a mistake.

## Practice prompts

- Tell me about a time Engineering disagreed with your recommendation.
- Describe a decision you made with incomplete data.
- Tell me about a time you changed your mind.
- Describe a time a stakeholder wanted something you believed was wrong for customers.
- Tell me about a missed target and what you learned.
- Describe how you helped a team align around a difficult trade-off.

## Review standard

A strong behavioural answer should reveal the situation, your ownership, your reasoning, your actions, the impact, and your learning. If the interviewer cannot tell what you personally did, the story needs more specificity.
