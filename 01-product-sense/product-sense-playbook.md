# Product Sense Playbook

## What Product Sense means in an interview

Product Sense is your ability to reason about people, problems, product experiences, and outcomes. It is not simply creativity. A useful answer demonstrates that you can move from a broad prompt to a well-justified product decision.

## The seven-step approach

### 1. Clarify the objective and boundaries

Ask questions that materially affect the answer:

- Are we designing a new product or improving an existing one?
- Which market or geography should we consider?
- Is there a priority business outcome?
- Are there constraints around time, technology, regulation, or distribution?

If the interviewer asks you to proceed, state two or three assumptions and continue.

### 2. Identify users and context

Do not say "everyone". Segment users by behaviour, need, context, frequency, ability, or job-to-be-done.

For each segment, consider:

- What are they trying to accomplish?
- How often do they do it?
- What triggers the need?
- What alternatives do they use?
- What makes the task difficult, risky, slow, or frustrating?

Choose a segment by explaining the opportunity: severity of need, frequency, reach, strategic fit, and ability to serve it.

### 3. Find the problem worth solving

Translate observations into a problem statement:

"[User segment] struggles to [complete a job] when [context], because [barrier], which leads to [consequence]."

This is a hypothesis, not a fact, unless the prompt supplies evidence. Say what you would validate through interviews, usability tests, support tickets, analytics, or field observation.

### 4. Define success before solutions

Choose an outcome that represents user value. Examples include task completion, time to complete a job, successful first use, repeat use, error rate, or customer retention.

Connect the user outcome to a business outcome where appropriate. Do not use downloads or clicks as a success measure unless they genuinely represent the goal.

### 5. Generate options

Create two or three different approaches. Vary the mechanism, not just the wording. For example: simplify the flow, provide guidance at the point of need, or change the underlying service process.

Consider:

- User value.
- Reach and frequency.
- Confidence in the problem.
- Implementation effort and dependencies.
- Risks, accessibility, privacy, and misuse.
- Strategic fit.

### 6. Choose and scope a first release

State what you would build first and what you would deliberately leave out. Describe the smallest version that tests the central assumption. Define a rollout plan and what must be true before expanding.

### 7. Measure and learn

Use:

- **Primary outcome:** the main user or business result.
- **Leading indicators:** behaviours that should move before the outcome.
- **Guardrails:** quality, trust, safety, cost, or reliability measures that must not deteriorate.
- **Segments:** who benefits and who may be harmed or excluded.

Explain what result would make you continue, iterate, or stop.

## How to answer an improvement question

1. Establish the product's job and target users.
2. Identify the most important journey or use case.
3. Name a likely friction point and state how you would validate it.
4. Prioritise one friction point.
5. Present a solution and a lower-cost alternative.
6. Define success and guardrails.

## How to answer a new-product question

1. Define the customer and problem space.
2. Estimate the need and alternatives, at a level appropriate to the timebox.
3. State the value proposition.
4. Describe the core workflow and differentiator.
5. Identify a narrow initial segment and distribution route.
6. Define a first test and metrics.

## How to recover when you get stuck

Pause and restate the objective. Ask: "Which user is most affected?" or "What assumption is driving my recommendation?" Then make the next decision explicit. It is better to reason clearly through one promising path than to list ten disconnected features.

## Practice drill

Pick an app you used today. In 15 minutes:

- Write its primary user and job.
- Draw the current journey in five steps.
- Identify one friction point.
- State the evidence you would inspect.
- Propose two solutions.
- Choose one and define one outcome plus two guardrails.

Repeat the exercise with a product you do not personally use. This tests whether you can reason beyond your own preferences.
