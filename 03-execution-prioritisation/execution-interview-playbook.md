# Execution Interview Playbook

## What execution questions test

Execution questions assess how you move from a goal to reliable delivery when time, people, and information are limited. They are not only project-management questions. They test whether you can protect outcomes while navigating trade-offs.

## A practical execution sequence

### 1. Reconfirm the outcome

Ask what must change for the customer or business. A date or feature request is not itself an outcome. Clarify whether the goal is fixed, negotiable, or a proxy for another need.

### 2. Understand the current state

Identify what is complete, what remains, what is uncertain, and where the risk sits. Talk to the people closest to the work. Avoid blaming a function before understanding the constraint.

### 3. Expose constraints and dependencies

Map:

- Scope and acceptance criteria.
- Engineering and design capacity.
- Data, platform, security, legal, or vendor dependencies.
- Operational readiness and support.
- Release, migration, and rollback requirements.
- External commitments and decision owners.

### 4. Create options

Common options include reducing scope, phasing delivery, changing sequence, adding capacity where effective, moving the date, using a manual operational workaround, or stopping lower-value work. Do not assume adding people makes work faster.

### 5. Compare options transparently

Evaluate user impact, business impact, risk, reversibility, effort, confidence, and opportunity cost. Make clear which constraint you are protecting.

### 6. Decide and assign ownership

State the recommendation, decision owner, accountable owners, key milestones, and escalation path. Define what is out of scope.

### 7. Communicate early

Tell affected stakeholders what changed, why, what the options were, what is now committed, what is at risk, and when the next update will happen. Do not present an uncertain date as guaranteed.

### 8. Close the loop

After delivery, review whether the intended outcome occurred, what created delay or quality issues, and what operating change would prevent recurrence.

## Prioritisation is a decision, not a score

Frameworks can help compare work, but they do not replace judgment. Use the same criteria across options, explain confidence, and preserve a space for mandatory work such as security, reliability, legal obligations, or critical customer commitments.

## Roadmap communication

A useful roadmap explains:

- Outcomes or problems being targeted.
- Why these matter now.
- The sequence and major dependencies.
- Confidence level and assumptions.
- What is deliberately not included.
- How progress and outcomes will be reviewed.

Avoid presenting every idea as a commitment. Separate committed work, planned work, and exploratory bets.

## Stakeholder conflict

When two stakeholders disagree:

1. Understand each person's goal and concern.
2. Separate the position they are advocating from the outcome they need.
3. Make constraints and evidence visible.
4. Present options and consequences.
5. Agree on a decision owner and deadline.
6. Document the decision and revisit condition.

Escalation should clarify a decision that cannot be resolved at the working level, not be used to transfer responsibility.

## Practice drill

You have one engineering team and three requests: a compliance deadline, a high-value customer workflow, and a conversion improvement. Create a decision table. Identify mandatory constraints, compare the remaining work, propose a sequence, and write a short stakeholder update.
